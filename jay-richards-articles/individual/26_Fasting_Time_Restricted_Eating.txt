Title: Fasting for Body and Soul, Week Two: Time-Restricted Eating
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: April 2018
Source: The Stream
URL: https://stream.org/fasting-time-restricted-eating/

This article is week two of Richards's plan to help readers adapt to a fasting lifestyle. Having laid the groundwork in the first week, Richards introduces time-restricted eating as the next step in building a sustainable fasting practice.

Time-restricted eating involves confining all food consumption to a specific window of time each day and fasting for the remainder. Richards recommends starting with a 16-hour fast -- meaning all eating occurs within an 8-hour window -- and then gradually extending the fasting period to 20 hours, leaving only a 4-hour eating window.

For most people, the easiest way to implement a 16-hour fast is to skip breakfast and eat only between noon and 8 PM, or a similar window. This approach works with most social and work schedules and is sustainable for long-term practice. As the body adapts to 16-hour fasts, extending to 18 or even 20 hours becomes progressively easier.

Richards discusses the growing body of scientific research supporting the health benefits of time-restricted eating. Studies have shown benefits including improved insulin sensitivity, reduced inflammation, enhanced cellular repair through autophagy, and improved metabolic markers. These physical benefits complement the spiritual benefits that Richards has discussed in his broader series on fasting.

The article emphasizes the importance of gradual adaptation. Jumping directly into extended fasts without preparation can be counterproductive, leading to discomfort, irritability, and failure. By building up gradually -- from skipping a meal, to a 16-hour fast, to longer fasts -- the body and mind adapt, and fasting becomes a natural part of one's routine rather than an ordeal.

Richards connects the physical practice of time-restricted eating to the broader spiritual discipline of fasting, arguing that caring for the body and caring for the soul are not separate endeavors but complementary aspects of a well-ordered life.
