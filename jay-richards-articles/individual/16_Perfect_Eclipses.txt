Title: Perfect Eclipses: Coincidence or Conspiracy?
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: September 2017
Source: The Stream
URL: https://stream.org/perfect-eclipses-coincidence-or-conspiracy/

A rare alignment allows Earthlings to witness "perfect" solar eclipses -- our Moon just barely covers the Sun's bright photosphere, revealing the otherwise invisible solar corona in a spectacular display. This precise match depends on just the right sizes, shapes, and relative distances of the Sun, Moon, and Earth, with no law of physics dictating this layout.

The Sun is approximately 400 times larger than the Moon, but it is also approximately 400 times farther away. This remarkable coincidence means that the two bodies appear almost exactly the same size in our sky. During a total solar eclipse, the Moon's disk covers the Sun's bright surface almost perfectly, leaving just the wispy corona visible around the edges. This is not something that had to be the case -- it is a contingent arrangement that could easily have been otherwise.

There are 65 major moons in our Solar System, but only we enjoy perfect solar eclipses. Other planets with moons do not have this precise size-distance relationship. Jupiter's moons, for instance, are either too large or too small relative to the Sun as seen from Jupiter's surface to produce the same effect. Earth's perfect eclipses are, as far as we know, unique in the Solar System.

Our ability to observe perfect solar eclipses has played a key role in several major scientific discoveries. The solar corona, which is only visible during total eclipses, has provided crucial data about the Sun's atmosphere and magnetic field. Arthur Eddington's observations during the 1919 total solar eclipse provided the first empirical confirmation of Einstein's general theory of relativity, by measuring the bending of starlight around the Sun. The element helium was first discovered in the solar spectrum during an eclipse.

Richards argues that this convergence of factors -- the precise arrangement that produces perfect eclipses on the one planet in the Solar System that also happens to have observers capable of appreciating and learning from them -- points beyond mere coincidence toward purpose and design in the cosmos.
