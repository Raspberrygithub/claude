Title: Don't Fear the Robots. Fear the Robot Philosophers.
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: 2018
Source: The Stream
URL: https://stream.org/dont-fear-the-robots-fear-the-robot-philosophers/

Richards promotes his book The Human Advantage, arguing that while automation and artificial intelligence will transform the economy, the real danger lies not in the machines themselves but in the philosophical errors of those who think about them.

In the short run, robots will disrupt how we do things but will also make us wealthier. Automation has always displaced certain kinds of work while creating new opportunities and raising overall living standards. The current wave of AI and robotics is no different in principle, even if the pace of change is faster than in previous technological revolutions.

Richards is far more worried about the bad thinking that leads academics to view man as a machine, and future machines as men. This philosophical confusion -- the tendency to reduce human beings to biological computers and to attribute human-like consciousness to artificial systems -- poses a deeper threat than any particular technological disruption.

He critiques Notre Dame philosophy professor Don Howard, who asked whether robots deserve human rights. Richards finds this question revealing -- not because it might have a surprising answer, but because of what it says about the questioner's assumptions. There is no more reason to think computers will become conscious than to think that strong tractors will become oxen. Computational power is not consciousness. Processing speed is not sentience. No matter how sophisticated a machine's behavior becomes, it remains a machine -- a tool designed and built by human beings.

Richards observes that Howard doesn't really argue robots should have human rights -- he argues neither robots nor humans have rights. This is the real danger of robot philosophy: it does not elevate machines to the level of humans but rather degrades humans to the level of machines. If consciousness is merely computation, then human beings have no special dignity, no inherent rights, and no moral status that a sufficiently advanced machine could not also claim.

The Human Advantage, Richards argues, lies not in our ability to compute but in our uniquely human capacities: creativity, moral reasoning, interpersonal connection, and the ability to find meaning and purpose. These are the qualities that no machine can replicate and that will remain valuable in any economy.
