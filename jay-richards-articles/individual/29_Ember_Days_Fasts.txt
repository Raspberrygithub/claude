Title: Let's Recover the Ember Days Fasts
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: September 2018
Source: The Stream
URL: https://stream.org/lets-recover-ember-days-fasts/

Richards calls for the recovery of the traditional Ember Days fasts -- quarterly periods of fasting and prayer that were once a standard feature of the Christian liturgical calendar but have largely fallen out of practice.

The article discusses Bishop Robert Morlino's letter and his pledge to keep the traditional Ember Days fasts. Bishop Morlino's commitment to this ancient practice, Richards argues, represents exactly the kind of liturgical recovery that the Church needs in a time of spiritual laxity and institutional crisis.

The Ember Days are sets of three days -- Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday -- that occur four times a year, roughly corresponding to the four seasons. They were traditionally observed with fasting and prayer, often associated with ordinations, the agricultural cycle, and thanksgiving for God's gifts. The practice dates back to at least the early centuries of Christianity and was universally observed in the Western Church for over a millennium.

Richards argues that the Ember Days fasts were abandoned not because they were found wanting but because of a broader tendency in modern Christianity to minimize or eliminate practices that require discipline and self-denial. The loss of the Ember Days is part of a larger pattern of liturgical impoverishment that has left many Christians without the rhythms of fasting and feasting that once structured the Christian year.

Recovering the Ember Days, Richards contends, would provide Christians with a regular, structured practice of fasting that connects them to the wisdom of the ancient Church. It would also provide natural occasions for prayer and reflection at the turning points of the year, reminding believers of their dependence on God for the gifts of creation and grace.

The article offers practical guidance for Christians who wish to observe the Ember Days, whether within a tradition that still formally recognizes them or as a personal or communal devotion.
