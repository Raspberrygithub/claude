Title: Medical Ethicist Warns: Beware the Idolatry of a COVID-19 Vaccine
Authors: Jay W. Richards and John Zmirak
Date: Undated
Source: The Stream
URL: https://stream.org/medical-ethicist-warns-beware-the-idolatry-of-a-covid-19-vaccine/

Co-authored with John Zmirak, this article raises ethical questions about the development of COVID-19 vaccines, particularly regarding the use of fetal cell lines derived from aborted fetuses in the research and production process.

The authors pose a provocative thought experiment: "What if most of the critical vaccines we needed to take throughout our lives had been developed using body parts from Uyghur slaves in China? Or victims of police shootings in America? How would Christians react?" The purpose of this analogy is to highlight the moral gravity of using cell lines derived from abortion in vaccine development -- a concern that many Christians share but that has been dismissed or minimized in public discourse.

Richards and Zmirak argue that the intense pressure to accept COVID-19 vaccines without questioning their ethical provenance represents a form of idolatry -- the elevation of health and safety above all other moral considerations. While health is a genuine good, it is not the highest good, and pursuing it at the expense of moral principles represents a disordered set of priorities.

The article examines the specific ethical concerns raised by the use of fetal cell lines in vaccine development. Some COVID-19 vaccines used cell lines derived from aborted fetuses in their testing or production processes. For Christians who believe that abortion is a grave moral wrong, participation in a medical system that benefits from and depends on abortion raises serious questions of moral complicity.

The authors acknowledge the complexity of the issue and the legitimate disagreements among Christians about the degree of moral cooperation involved. They do not argue that all Christians must refuse vaccination. Rather, they argue that Christians should take these ethical concerns seriously, resist the social pressure to dismiss them, and insist on their right to make conscientious decisions about their own medical care.

The article calls for greater investment in ethical vaccine development that does not rely on morally compromised cell lines, and for respect for the conscience rights of those who object to existing vaccines on religious or ethical grounds.
