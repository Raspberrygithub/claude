Title: How Cosmos Does Religious History Badly
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: April 2014
Source: The Federalist
URL: https://thefederalist.com/2014/04/30/how-cosmos-does-religious-history-badly/

Richards critiques the FOX/National Geographic Cosmos series, hosted by Neil deGrasse Tyson, for its handling of the history of science and religion. The show spent a quarter of its first episode on a misleading story about Giordano Bruno, presenting him as a martyr for science persecuted by the Catholic Church.

The problem, Richards argues, is that Bruno was not a scientist and had virtually nothing to do with the history of science. Bruno was a Dominican friar, philosopher, and mystic whose cosmological speculations were rooted in mysticism and magic, not empirical observation or mathematical reasoning. His ideas about an infinite universe with many worlds were not based on scientific evidence but on his idiosyncratic theological and philosophical views.

Bruno was indeed burned at the stake by the Roman Inquisition in 1600, and his execution was a grave injustice by any standard. But he was not executed for his scientific views -- he was executed for theological heresies, including denying the divinity of Christ and other core Christian doctrines. His cosmological speculations played a minor role, if any, in his trial and condemnation.

By presenting Bruno as a scientific martyr, Cosmos perpetuates the "conflict thesis" -- the idea that science and religion have been locked in perpetual warfare, with religion always on the side of ignorance and oppression. This narrative, Richards argues, is a historical myth that has been thoroughly debunked by historians of science. The actual relationship between science and Christianity has been far more complex and far more positive than the conflict thesis suggests.

Richards points out that many of the greatest scientists in history -- including Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler, Newton, and Faraday -- were devout Christians whose faith motivated and informed their scientific work. A show purporting to tell the history of science should represent this history accurately rather than distorting it to serve a contemporary ideological agenda.
