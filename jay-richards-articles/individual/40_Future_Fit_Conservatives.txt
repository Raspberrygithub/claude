Title: A Future Fit for Conservatives
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: January 2024
Source: Acton Blog
URL: https://blog.acton.org/archives/125224-a-future-fit-for-conservatives.html

Richards reflects on the future of the conservative movement and the principles that should guide it going forward. Writing for the Acton Institute's blog, he considers how conservatives can remain faithful to enduring principles while adapting to the challenges of a rapidly changing world.

The article addresses the tension within the conservative movement between those who emphasize free markets and limited government and those who prioritize cultural and social conservatism. Richards argues that these two strands are not in tension but are complementary -- that a healthy society requires both economic freedom and strong cultural institutions, and that neither can thrive without the other.

Richards contends that conservatives must resist the temptation to abandon core principles in pursuit of short-term political gains. The principles of limited government, individual liberty, the rule of law, and the importance of mediating institutions -- family, church, and community -- are not relics of a bygone era but enduring truths about human nature and the conditions for human flourishing.

At the same time, conservatives must be willing to apply these principles to new challenges. The rise of Big Tech, the dominance of ideological institutions, the erosion of the family, and the growing power of the administrative state all require fresh thinking and creative responses. A conservatism that merely defends the status quo of 20 years ago is not truly conservative -- it is nostalgic.

Richards calls for a conservatism that is rooted in permanent principles but forward-looking in its application, confident in its convictions but humble in its approach, and willing to engage with the full range of challenges facing American society. Such a conservatism, he argues, would be well-positioned to offer a compelling alternative to the progressive vision that currently dominates American institutions.
