Title: Crusade Myths
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: February 2015
Source: The Stream
URL: https://stream.org/crusade-myths/

Richards responded to President Obama's Prayer Breakfast speech in which the President invoked the Crusades as if they were the moral equivalent of modern Islamist extremism. Obama suggested that Christians should get off their "high horse" about Islamic terrorism because of the violence committed in the name of Christianity during the Crusades and Inquisition.

Richards argues that this comparison reflects a deeply distorted understanding of history. The popular narrative of the Crusades -- that they were unprovoked wars of aggression by European Christians against peaceful Muslims -- is a myth that does not withstand historical scrutiny. The Crusades were, at their origin, a defensive response to centuries of Muslim conquest and expansion. By the time of the First Crusade in 1095, Muslim armies had conquered roughly two-thirds of the Christian world, including the Holy Land, North Africa, Spain, and much of the Middle East.

The medieval Crusaders were far from perfect, and atrocities were committed on all sides. But equating the Crusades with modern jihadist terrorism -- suicide bombings, beheadings, and the deliberate targeting of civilians -- is historically illiterate. The Crusades were complex military campaigns with mixed motives, conducted under the norms of medieval warfare. Modern Islamist terrorism is a contemporary phenomenon with its own distinct ideology and methods.

Richards contends that invoking the Crusades as a rhetorical weapon against Christians serves to deflect attention from the real and present threat of Islamist extremism. It also reflects a broader tendency in Western culture toward self-flagellation -- the impulse to blame Western civilization for the world's problems while minimizing or excusing the actions of others.

The article calls for a more honest engagement with history, one that neither whitewashes the Crusades nor distorts them into a convenient tool for contemporary political arguments.
