Title: Here's What Young People Need To Hear: Don't 'Follow Your Passion'
Author: Jay W. Richards
Date: June 2018
Source: The Federalist
URL: https://thefederalist.com/2018/06/15/heres-message-young-americans-need-hear-dont-follow-passion/

"Follow your passion" sounds wise, but it's a better fit for speeches than for a competitive job market or the pursuit of happiness. Richards argues that this ubiquitous piece of advice, dispensed at countless graduation ceremonies, is actively harmful to the young people who take it seriously.

The follow-your-passion myth assumes that our "passions" are fixed -- that somewhere inside each of us is a pre-existing passion waiting to be discovered, and that happiness consists in finding it and building a career around it. In the real world, our interests wax and wane. What fascinates us at 18 may bore us at 25. What we think we are passionate about often turns out to be a romanticized image of a field rather than the reality of daily work in it.

Richards references Mike Rowe, the host of Dirty Jobs, who offers a blunter assessment: "Just because you're passionate about something doesn't mean you won't suck at it." Passion without competence is a recipe for frustration, not fulfillment. And competence in a field that nobody needs is not much better.

Instead of following your passion, Richards advises young people to cultivate the desire to meet other people's needs, and then the passion and happiness will come. This approach reverses the conventional wisdom. Rather than starting with what you want and hoping the world will pay you for it, start with what the world needs and develop the skills to provide it. Passion, Richards argues, tends to follow mastery and service rather than preceding them.

This perspective is grounded in a richer understanding of human flourishing than the passion myth provides. True happiness comes not from self-expression but from contribution -- from using one's talents and skills to serve others and create genuine value. When young people orient themselves toward meeting real needs, they develop competence, earn the respect of others, and discover a deeper and more durable form of satisfaction than the fleeting excitement of "following their passion."
