Title: Why COVID-19 May Be Less Deadly Than We Thought
Authors: Jay W. Richards, Douglas Axe, and William Briggs
Date: March 2020
Source: The Stream
URL: https://stream.org/why-covid-19-may-be-less-deadly-than-we-thought/

Co-authored with Douglas Axe and William Briggs, this article questions the early estimates of COVID-19's lethality and the sweeping measures adopted in response.

"We've put our lives on hold until further notice." The authors observe that the response to COVID-19 has been unprecedented in its scope and severity. Rather than quarantining at-risk groups and advising public precautions -- the traditional public health approach to respiratory viruses -- we have shut down tens of thousands of businesses, churches, schools, and universities. The entire population has been subjected to restrictions previously reserved for the most extreme circumstances.

The authors argue that these measures "could exact a steep price in American lives and well-being." The costs of lockdowns are not merely economic, though the economic costs are enormous. They include delayed medical care for other conditions, increased mental health problems, substance abuse, domestic violence, and the educational and developmental harm to children cut off from school, friends, and normal social interaction.

The article examines the available data on COVID-19's infection fatality rate and argues that early estimates were likely inflated. Because testing was limited, the number of confirmed cases vastly underrepresented the true number of infections. This meant that the case fatality rate -- the ratio of deaths to confirmed cases -- overstated the actual risk of death for an infected person. The true infection fatality rate, the authors contend, was likely much lower than the alarming figures being cited in media reports.

Richards, Axe, and Briggs call for a more proportionate response -- one that protects the elderly and immunocompromised while allowing the broader population to resume normal life. They argue that the blanket shutdown approach is not only disproportionate to the actual risk but actively harmful to public health and well-being.
