## 2. Summary of Main Themes by U.S. Company

### X (formerly Twitter) — Most Heavily Penalized

**Enforcement themes:**
- **Dark patterns / deceptive design:** The "blue checkmark" verification system was found to deceive users. Previously a free indicator of authenticity, it became a €7/month subscription with no meaningful identity verification, misleading users about account credibility.
- **Advertising transparency failures:** X's ad repository lacked required information (advertiser identity, ad content/topic) and imposed excessive access barriers (delays, CAPTCHAs).
- **Researcher data access obstruction:** X's terms of service prohibited researchers from independently scraping public data, and its processes imposed unnecessary barriers to legitimate research.
- **Illegal content and disinformation:** An ongoing investigation examines how X handles illegal content, information manipulation, and algorithmic radicalization via recommender systems.

**Content types targeted:** Disinformation, hate speech (though X reported very low numbers: only 11 hate speech instances and 41 misinformation instances on a single reporting day in Nov 2023 — far lower than peers).

---

### Meta (Facebook & Instagram) — Second Most Targeted

**Enforcement themes:**
- **Deceptive advertising and disinformation:** The first proceeding (Apr 2024) focused on deceptive ads, political content visibility, and how Meta handles disinformation on its platforms.
- **CrowdTangle phase-out:** The Commission investigated Meta's decision to discontinue CrowdTangle, a tool widely used by researchers and journalists to track viral content and misinformation, raising concerns about reduced public oversight.
- **Researcher data access:** The second proceeding found Meta implemented burdensome procedures that gave researchers only partial or unreliable data.
- **Illegal content mechanisms:** Meta was found to lack simple mechanisms for users to report illegal content and challenge content moderation decisions.

**Content types targeted:** Adult nudity/sexual activity (66.6M enforcement actions in late 2024 — roughly double the previous quarter), hate speech (16M pieces flagged/removed in Q1 2024), misinformation. Meta received 248,748 user notices in a single reporting period on Facebook alone.

**Notable policy shift (Jan 2025):** Meta announced it would "undo mission creep" making rules "too restrictive," rolling back restrictions on topics like immigration, gender identity, and gender that are frequently debated in political discourse.

---

### Google / Alphabet (Search, YouTube, Play, Maps, Shopping)

**Enforcement themes:**
- **Researcher data access:** Part of the mass RFI sent to all 17 VLOPs in January 2024.
- **App Store systemic risks:** Specific RFI about how Google Play identifies and manages systemic risks.
- **AI-generated content:** RFI about measures to address dissemination of generative AI content.
- **Financial scams:** September 2025 RFI about how Google Search and Play manage fraudulent listings, apps, and ads leading to scam websites.

**Content types targeted:** YouTube's transparency reports show intellectual property infringement and privacy violations as top moderation categories (beyond the catch-all "scope of platform service"). YouTube reported 0 instances of illegal/harmful speech on a sample day — compared to TikTok's high volumes.

**No formal proceedings opened** as of early 2026.

---

### Apple (App Store)

**Enforcement themes:**
- **App Store systemic risks:** RFI about how Apple identifies systemic risks in the App Store ecosystem.
- **Financial scams:** September 2025 RFI about fraudulent banking/investment/trading apps mimicking legitimate ones.
- **Researcher data access:** Part of the January 2024 mass RFI.

**Content types targeted:** Primarily focused on fraudulent/scam apps rather than speech-related content.

**No formal proceedings opened.** Apple's main EU regulatory battles have been under the DMA (Digital Markets Act), not the DSA — including a €500M DMA fine in April 2025.

---

### Amazon (Amazon Store)

**Enforcement themes:**
- **Researcher data access:** Part of the January 2024 mass RFI.
- **Systemic risks:** General compliance inquiries.

**Content types targeted:** Product safety, counterfeit goods (as a marketplace, Amazon's DSA concerns are more about illegal products than speech content).

**No formal proceedings opened.**

---

### Microsoft (Bing, LinkedIn)

**Enforcement themes:**
- **AI-generated content:** RFI about measures against generative AI content dissemination.
- **Financial scams:** September 2025 RFI about how Bing manages links and ads leading to fraudulent websites.
- **LinkedIn and minors:** Scrutiny over targeted advertising using sensitive data and protection of minors.

**Content types targeted:** Financial fraud, AI-generated deceptive content, targeted advertising concerns.

**No formal proceedings opened.** The LinkedIn investigation (based on an EDRi complaint) appears to have concluded after the initial RFI stage.

## 3. Political Censorship & Conservative Speech Concerns

### The Core Allegation

The U.S. House Judiciary Committee (Republican majority) published an interim report titled **"The Foreign Censorship Threat"** alleging the DSA functions as a censorship mechanism that specifically targets conservative political speech. Key claims:

1. **"We need to take back our country"** — The Commission's own training workshop allegedly labeled this common political statement as "illegal hate speech" requiring platform censorship under the DSA.

2. **One-sided enforcement:** The report alleges censorship requests are "largely one-sided, almost uniformly targeting political conservatives," particularly on:
   - **Immigration policy** (e.g., German officials classified calls for deportation of criminal aliens as "incitement to hatred")
   - **Environmental policy** (e.g., Poland's National Research Institute flagged a TikTok post stating "electric cars are neither ecological nor an economical solution")
   - **Counter-terrorism commentary** (e.g., French authorities ordered removal of immigration commentary following a terrorist attack by a Syrian refugee)

3. **Structural incentive for over-removal:** With fines up to 6% of global turnover for failing to act on flagged content, platforms have a financial incentive to over-moderate — removing borderline-but-lawful political speech rather than risk massive penalties. This is called **"collateral censorship"** or the **"chilling effect."**

4. **"Systemic risk" as a censorship vector:** The DSA requires platforms to mitigate "systemic risks" including "information manipulation." Critics argue this gives regulators a mandate to target populist or dissident political messaging under the guise of risk management.

### Specific Evidence of Political Bias

- **Hate speech enforcement dropped 75%** from Q2 2024 to Q1 2025 (only a quarter of the comment removal volume), coinciding with political pushback and the U.S. election cycle — suggesting enforcement may be politically responsive.
- **Meta's January 2025 policy reversal** explicitly acknowledged that its content moderation rules had been "too restrictive" and "too prone to over-enforcement" on topics like immigration and gender identity — core conservative policy issues.
- **Terminology shifts:** Several platforms replaced "misinformation" and "disinformation" with the less charged "misleading information," suggesting awareness that the original framing carried political connotations.

### U.S. Government Response

- **VP JD Vance** publicly denounced the €120M X fine as EU censorship.
- **Secretary of State Marco Rubio** called the DSA "Orwellian."
- The **State Department** launched a diplomatic campaign pressuring EU officials to repeal or amend the DSA.
- The administration reportedly **contemplated sanctions** against European officials implementing the DSA.

### EU Rebuttal

The European Commission called the allegations "pure nonsense" and "completely unfounded," stating:
- "Freedom of expression is a fundamental right in Europe"
- The DSA concerns consumer protection, transparency, and risk mitigation — not political speech
- The X fine was about deceptive design (blue checkmarks) and transparency failures, not content censorship

### Assessment

The evidence supports a **structural concern**: the DSA's penalty framework (up to 6% global revenue) creates rational incentives for platforms to over-moderate, and the vague category of "systemic risk" gives regulators wide discretion. Whether this discretion has been systematically wielded against conservative viewpoints is contested — the Republican report cites specific examples, while the EU and independent analysts argue the regulatory intent is content-neutral. Meta's own admission of "mission creep" and "over-enforcement" on politically charged topics lends credibility to the concern that platforms were censoring lawful political speech, though it is unclear how much of that was driven by DSA compliance vs. pre-existing internal policies.

## 4. Trusted Flaggers: Who They Are, Who Funds Them

### How the System Works

Under **Article 22** of the DSA, national Digital Services Coordinators (DSCs) designate "trusted flaggers" — organizations whose content reports are treated with **priority** by platforms. Only **EU-based** entities can be designated. There are no U.S.-based trusted flaggers (the system is exclusively European). However, some organizations have international affiliations.

### Known Designated Trusted Flaggers

| Organization | Country | Focus Area | Funding Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HateAid** | Germany | Online hate speech | German government (€424,823 in 2025) |
| **Respect (Reporting Centre)** | Germany | Online hate | **95% German government-funded** (Demokratie Leben programme, Ministry of Family Affairs) |
| **Federal Association of Online Retailers** | Germany | Consumer protection / illegal products | Industry association |
| **Federal Association of Consumer Organizations (vzbv)** | Germany | Consumer protection | Government + membership fees |
| **Point de Contact** | France | Various illegal content | Mixed (NGO) |
| **ALPA** | France | Copyright / anti-piracy | Industry-funded (film/TV) |
| **IFAW** | France | Animal welfare / illegal wildlife trade | Donations / NGO |
| **INDECOSA-CGT** | France | Consumer protection | Trade union-affiliated |
| **Addictions France** | France | Addiction-related content | Government / health sector |
| **Central Bank of Ireland** | Ireland | Financial fraud | Government (regulatory body) |
| **Unia** | Belgium | Discrimination / hate speech | Government-funded equality body |
| **INHOPE network members** | Multiple EU states | Child sexual abuse material (CSAM) | EU Commission (Digital Europe Programme) + platform funding from Meta, Google, TikTok |

### Independence Concerns

This is a critical issue. Key findings:

1. **Government funding dominance:** Organizations like Respect (95% state-funded) and HateAid (fully government-funded) are effectively **semi-governmental** actors making quasi-judicial decisions about what content is "illegal."

2. **Platform funding conflicts:** INHOPE network members receive funding from Meta, Google, and TikTok — the very platforms they flag content on. While INHOPE focuses on CSAM (less politically controversial), the structural conflict of interest is notable.

3. **No scrutiny of state independence:** German former BSW member Pürner noted that the network agency examined independence from online platforms, but "there was no critical scrutiny of state independence." The DSA requires independence from platforms but does not require independence from governments.

4. **Lawyer Ralf Höcker's warning:** "Trusted flaggers are controversial because they are semi-governmental organisations that are financed by the state with large sums of money and de facto decide what is legal and what is not."

5. **CDU lawmaker Saskia Ludwig:** Argued that entrusting private organisations receiving up to 95% state funding to police online speech poses serious risks to freedom of expression.

### Connection to U.S. Concerns

While no U.S. organizations are directly designated as trusted flaggers, the **"Brussels Effect"** means these decisions impact American users. When European trusted flaggers report content, platforms often apply moderation decisions globally rather than geo-restricting them. Content created by Americans can be removed or restricted based on determinations by European government-funded organizations about what constitutes "illegal" speech under EU standards.

### Key Observations on the Timeline

1. **Pre-DSA baseline (before Apr 2023):** Zero formal enforcement actions. Platform content moderation was entirely self-regulated or governed by individual national laws (e.g., Germany's NetzDG).

2. **Phase 1 — Information Gathering (Oct 2023 – Jan 2024):** ~24 RFIs sent across all platforms. No formal proceedings yet. The Commission was building its evidentiary base.

3. **Phase 2 — Proceedings Opened (Feb – Oct 2024):** Formal proceedings launched against X, TikTok (3x), Meta, AliExpress, and Temu. A sharp escalation from information requests to adversarial investigations.

4. **Phase 3 — Preliminary Findings & First Fine (2025):** 6 sets of preliminary findings issued. The first fine (€120M against X) was imposed in December 2025. Adult content platforms (Pornhub, XVideos, XNXX) were targeted for the first time.

5. **The trend is unambiguously upward** — from zero actions pre-DSA, to information-gathering in late 2023, to formal proceedings in 2024, to preliminary findings and the first fine in 2025. Multiple additional fines are expected in 2026.
