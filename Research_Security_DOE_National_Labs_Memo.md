# MEMORANDUM

**RE: Research Security Risks Posed by Foreign Nationals from Countries of Risk at DOE National Laboratories**

**Hearing:** U.S. Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources
**Date:** February 20, 2025
**Location:** Room 366, Dirksen Senate Office Building, Washington, D.C.
**Chairman:** Senator Mike Lee (R-UT)

---

## Executive Summary

On February 20, 2025, the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources convened a hearing examining espionage and research security vulnerabilities at the Department of Energy's 17 national laboratories. Three former senior DOE officials—Dr. Geraldine Richmond, Anna B. Puglisi, and Paul M. Dabbar—testified on the scope of the threat, existing mitigation frameworks, and the need for expanded legislative action. The hearing revealed that in FY2023 alone, approximately 40,000 foreign scientists accessed DOE laboratories, with nearly 8,000 from China and Russia—meaning **one in every five foreign scientists entering America's top labs came from adversarial nations**. All three witnesses and the Committee agreed that current protections are insufficient, though they differed on the appropriate balance between security restrictions and the scientific openness that underpins American technological leadership.

---

## I. Witness Testimony Summaries

### A. Dr. Geraldine L. Richmond
**Presidential Chair in Science and Professor of Chemistry, University of Oregon; Former Under Secretary for Science and Innovation, DOE (2021–2025)**

Dr. Richmond testified from the perspective of having directly overseen 13 DOE national laboratories and the entire DOE Office of Science portfolio—the nation's largest federal sponsor of basic research in the physical sciences. Her testimony focused on the risk management architecture she helped build and a stark warning about new vulnerabilities created by workforce disruptions.

**Key points:**

- **S&T Risk Matrix:** Richmond detailed DOE's Science & Technology Risk Matrix, authorized under the CHIPS and Science Act, which categorizes emerging research areas using a **Red/Yellow/Green** framework. "Red" topics are restricted emerging technologies whose disclosure to countries of risk could cause significant harm to critical U.S. national interests. The Matrix applies to all national laboratory international transactions—including foreign national access requests, travel to countries of risk on restricted topics, and engagement requests. It was updated in December 2022 by the National Laboratory Chief Research Officer group and is intended to be a "living document."

- **Talent Recruitment Program Bans:** In 2019, DOE prohibited all personnel and laboratory management and operating (M&O) contractors from participating in Talent Recruitment Programs sponsored by countries of concern (China, Russia, Iran, North Korea). In 2020, this was expanded to cover all "Other Foreign Government Sponsored or Affiliated Activities," including employment, in-kind contributions, grants, awards, funding, scholarships, and appointments—with participation requiring direct approval by the Secretary of Energy.

- **Legislative Framework:** Richmond described a multi-layered approach built on the CHIPS and Science Act, the SBIR and STTR Extension Act of 2022, NDAAs for FYs 2020, 2021, and 2025, and Presidential Directive NSPM-33. With $62 billion in programs under DOE's purview through the CHIPS and Science Act, the Department emphasized a comprehensive approach to research, technology, and economic security (RTES) policy for all financial assistance awards and loans.

- **Workforce Vulnerability Warning:** Richmond warned that the Trump administration's mass layoffs and budget cuts, combined with the rigorous and time-consuming background check requirements for classified work, could make displaced personnel **more vulnerable to foreign recruitment**. She disclosed that she herself had been recruited by China, underscoring that the threat is persistent and personal even at the highest levels of DOE leadership.

---

### B. Anna B. Puglisi
**Visiting Fellow, Hoover Institution, Stanford University; Former National Counterintelligence Officer for East Asia**

Puglisi brought the perspective of a career intelligence professional. She previously served as the National Counterintelligence Officer for East Asia, advising senior U.S. and foreign government officials at the highest levels, and played a prominent role in drafting the U.S. National Counterintelligence Strategy. Her testimony focused on China's systematic, state-directed approach to acquiring foreign technology and on building institutional capacity to detect and counter these efforts.

**Key points:**

- **"Pre-Check" System:** Puglisi proposed creating a national "pre-check" system—analogous to the TSA PreCheck model—to streamline collaborations with researchers who have been vetted and adhere to clear security standards. This would reduce friction for legitimate collaborators while concentrating security resources on higher-risk engagements.

- **National Open-Source Information Center:** Puglisi and Richmond jointly recommended establishing a national center for open-source information to help universities and laboratories make more informed decisions about potential research security threats. This would provide institutions with actionable intelligence without requiring classified access.

- **International Framework:** Puglisi advocated for creating an international framework of research expectations and security standards for visiting students and scientists—a multilateral approach that would establish norms and provide a basis for denying access when standards are not met.

- **China's Systematic Approach:** Drawing on her intelligence career, Puglisi emphasized that China's technology acquisition strategy is not opportunistic but systematic and state-directed, operating through talent recruitment programs, joint ventures, academic partnerships, and direct intelligence collection.

---

### C. Paul M. Dabbar
**CEO and Co-Founder, Bohr Quantum Technology; Former Under Secretary for Science, DOE (2017–2021); Subsequently Nominated as Deputy Secretary of Commerce (2025)**

Dabbar delivered the most forceful testimony, calling for a default ban on Chinese nationals at all 17 national laboratories. His testimony drew on his direct experience overseeing DOE labs during the Trump administration's first term, when the espionage threat from China was escalating.

**Key points:**

- **Default Ban with Waivers:** Dabbar recommended that DOE implement a default prohibition on Chinese nationals at all national laboratories—not just the three NNSA labs covered by the FY2025 NDAA—with the Department able to grant waivers on a case-by-case basis. He criticized existing DOE orders as providing "too much flexibility and discretion."

- **Chinese National Security Law:** Dabbar emphasized a foundational legal reality: "We must also remember that all PRC citizens are required by the Chinese National Security Law to hand over all information when directed by the Chinese state." This statute, enacted in 2017, effectively makes every Chinese citizen a potential compulsory intelligence asset, regardless of their personal intentions.

- **Stricter Authorization Controls:** Dabbar recommended that all decisions regarding hiring, visits, and meetings involving nationals from high-risk countries be authorized solely by lab under secretaries without delegation, and that DOE establish a defined list of allowable waivers subject to Congressional oversight.

- **A Generation of Theft:** Dabbar characterized the scope of the problem bluntly: "There's been literally a whole generation of successful efforts by Communist China on stealing stuff."

- **Expansion Beyond NNSA Labs:** The FY2025 NDAA's restrictions cover only the three NNSA national security labs (Los Alamos, Lawrence Livermore, Sandia). Dabbar argued these restrictions must extend to all 17 DOE national laboratories, which conduct cutting-edge research on energy, advanced computing, artificial intelligence, quantum science, and other dual-use technologies.

## II. The Threat Landscape: Key Statistics and Evidence

### Scale of Foreign Access to DOE Laboratories

| Metric | Figure | Source |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Foreign scientists visiting DOE labs annually | ~40,000 | Sen. Cotton's office / GATE Act introduction |
| PRC nationals visiting 16 national labs (FY2023) | >7,000 | Intelligence Authorization Act for FY2024, Joint Explanatory Statement |
| Chinese and Russian nationals visiting labs (FY2023) | ~8,000 | Sen. Cotton's office |
| Share of foreign lab visitors from adversarial nations | ~1 in 5 (20%) | Sen. Cotton's office |

### Documented Espionage and Recruitment Cases

- **Strider Technologies Report (2021):** At least **162 former Los Alamos National Laboratory scientists** were recruited by Beijing to work on Chinese military programs over the past three decades. The report identified systematic targeting of scientists with knowledge of advanced weapons systems, nuclear technology, and defense applications.

- **Zhao Yusheng Case:** A scientist who received nearly **$20 million in U.S. taxpayer grants** during an 18-year career at Los Alamos, where he held a **top-secret Q clearance** and led a defense project developing bombs designed to penetrate deep underground. In 2016, Zhao joined a Chinese talent program and left the United States. Officials stated Los Alamos is "by no means an outlier."

- **DOE Knowledge of Recruitment:** The Department of Energy has documented situations in which individuals have been offered **hundreds of thousands or even millions of dollars** to conduct research on behalf of foreign talent recruitment programs while simultaneously receiving support from U.S. agencies. DOE has also identified laboratory personnel recruited by talent programs who subsequently became affiliated with foreign military R&D programs.

- **Charles Lieber (Harvard):** Convicted in December 2021 of lying to federal authorities about his participation in China's Thousand Talents Program and failing to report income from Chinese institutions—demonstrating that the threat extends beyond national labs into the broader research ecosystem.

- **Hongjin Tan:** Applied to China's Thousand Talents Program and stole **$1 billion worth of proprietary chemical formulas** for battery technology. Sentenced to 24 months in prison. Investigators revealed Tan had pledged to "digest and absorb" U.S. technology on behalf of Chinese entities.

- **Aggregate Enforcement:** Across the U.S. research enterprise, **54 scientists have lost their jobs** for failing to disclose external funding from China, and **more than 20 have been charged** on espionage and fraud allegations (as of 2025).

### China's Talent Recruitment Programs

- China's talent recruitment programs drew in nearly **60,000 overseas professionals** between 2008 and 2016 (official Chinese statistics).
- Within a decade of the Thousand Talents Plan's 2008 launch, it had attracted more than **7,000 participants** overall.
- In 2019, DOE adopted a rule prohibiting employees and contractors from participating in talent programs linked to China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea. The rule appears to have reduced the brain drain, but enforcement challenges persist.

### Intelligence Community Assessments

- The Senate Intelligence Committee found that DOE's Office of Intelligence and Counterintelligence (DOE-IN) **does not require comprehensive screening and vetting** of foreign visitors or assignees from the PRC, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and Cuba who work or collaborate with scientists at the National Laboratories.
- The Intelligence Committees directed DOE-IN to implement a robust screening and vetting regime and ensure appropriate oversight to identify and track counterintelligence threat information.
- A **2025 National Counterintelligence and Security Center (NCSC) report** confirmed that Chinese intelligence services continue to recruit students, steal American research, and engage in harassment of dissidents at U.S. universities—activities that directly overlap with the national laboratory ecosystem.

### Lack of Reciprocity

Senator Tom Cotton posed a pointed question during the hearing: **"Do you think one out of every five foreign scientists in a Chinese or Russian equivalent site is American?"** He answered his own question: **"There is zero reciprocity on this issue."** China's national laboratories and defense research institutions are closed systems with no comparable foreign access, while U.S. labs operate on principles of scientific openness that adversaries have systematically exploited.

## III. Current Legal and Policy Framework

### FY2025 National Defense Authorization Act (Section 3112)
Signed into law by President Biden in December 2024, this provision:
- **Prohibits** citizens or agents of China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea from accessing non-public areas of DOE's national security laboratories and nuclear weapons production facilities
- **Effective date:** April 15, 2025
- **Scope:** Limited to three NNSA labs—Los Alamos National Laboratory, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, and Sandia National Laboratories
- **Waivers:** Permitted with Secretarial authorization
- **Reporting:** Quarterly reports to Congress on covered foreign nationals beginning 90 days after enactment
- **Note:** This was a pared-down version of a broader Senate Intelligence Committee proposal that would have applied to all 17 DOE national labs

### DOE S&T Risk Matrix
- Authorized under the CHIPS and Science Act
- Uses Red/Yellow/Green categorization to assess risk levels for emerging research topics
- Red topics: restricted; require enhanced vetting and Secretarial approval for engagement with countries of concern
- Yellow topics: require additional controls and oversight
- Green topics: open collaboration permitted where DOE assesses net benefit to U.S. scientific enterprise
- Countries of concern: China, Russia, Iran, North Korea
- Applies to all national laboratory international transactions

### Talent Recruitment Program Restrictions
- **2019:** DOE prohibited participation in Talent Recruitment Programs from countries of concern
- **2020:** Expanded to all "Other Foreign Government Sponsored or Affiliated Activities" from countries of concern; participation requires Secretary of Energy approval
- Covers: employment, in-kind contributions, grants, awards, funding, scholarships, and appointments

### NSPM-33 (National Security Presidential Memorandum)
Presidential directive requiring enhanced disclosure of foreign funding and affiliations by researchers receiving federal grants, with standardized requirements across agencies.

## IV. Proposed Legislation: The GATE Act

Following the February 20 hearing, Senator Tom Cotton introduced the **Guarding American Technology from Exploitation (GATE) Act**, cosponsored by Senators Mike Lee (R-UT), John Barrasso (R-WY), Susan Collins (R-ME), and James Lankford (R-OK).

**Key provisions:**
- **Scope:** Prohibits non-citizen researchers from China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and Cuba (adding Cuba beyond the NDAA's four countries) from visiting or working at **all 17 DOE national laboratories**—not just the three NNSA labs
- **Waiver mechanism:** The Secretary of Energy may grant waivers for academic visitors if they demonstrate that the benefits of their presence "outweigh the national security and economic risks to the United States"
- **Intelligence community role:** Waivers require coordination with the intelligence community
- **Unaffected:** U.S. citizens and permanent residents are not subject to the ban
- **Legislative history:** A similar version passed the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence by a vote of **17-0** in the previous Congress but was not included in the final NDAA

**Opposition:**
- DOE has pushed back, stating the proposal "would have a significant impact on our national laboratories"
- Critics warn that "the provision would severely limit our ability to engage with Chinese and Russian experts on nonproliferation of biological, chemical and nuclear weapons"
- Some scientists argue the restrictions could drive talent to competitor nations and undermine the collaborative model that has been central to American scientific preeminence

## V. Post-Hearing Developments

Following the hearing, Chairman Lee sent letters to the Directors of **Oak Ridge, Los Alamos, and Argonne National Laboratories** regarding reports that researchers at all three labs had engaged in research collaborations leveraging **PRC-based supercomputers—including those linked to the PRC's military**—for federally funded research in sensitive fields. This revelation underscored that the security risks extend beyond physical presence at laboratories to include digital and computational collaborations that may circumvent existing access controls.

## VI. Analysis: Core Tensions

### Security vs. Scientific Openness
The fundamental tension at the heart of this hearing is the conflict between the imperative to protect classified and sensitive research from adversarial exploitation and the scientific openness that has been the foundation of American technological leadership. The national laboratory system was built on principles of international collaboration, peer review, and the free exchange of ideas. Restricting access risks degrading precisely the qualities that make these institutions valuable.

### Targeted vs. Blanket Restrictions
The witnesses represented a spectrum of approaches:
- **Dabbar** favored broad, default bans with narrow waivers—prioritizing security with a high bar for exceptions
- **Richmond** emphasized the risk management architecture (S&T Risk Matrix, talent program bans) that allows nuanced, topic-by-topic risk assessment rather than blanket nationality-based restrictions
- **Puglisi** proposed systemic solutions (pre-check systems, open-source intelligence centers, international frameworks) that would improve the quality of security decisions rather than simply restricting access

### Workforce Vulnerability
Richmond's warning about budget cuts and layoffs creating new recruitment vulnerabilities deserves particular attention. Displaced scientists with classified knowledge and security clearances are high-value targets for foreign intelligence services. The intersection of fiscal austerity with security imperatives creates a paradox: the very measures intended to reduce government spending may increase the vulnerability of sensitive knowledge to adversarial acquisition.

### The Reciprocity Gap
The lack of reciprocity between the U.S. and China is a structural asymmetry that pervades this issue. The United States opens its premier research institutions to foreign nationals on a scale that has no parallel in Chinese, Russian, or Iranian practice. Whether this openness represents a strategic vulnerability to be closed or a competitive advantage to be protected is the central policy question.

---

## VII. Key Takeaways

1. **The threat is documented, quantified, and ongoing.** With 162 recruited scientists from a single laboratory, $20 million in taxpayer funds flowing to a scientist who defected to China's talent programs, and 8,000 nationals from adversarial nations accessing labs annually, the scale of the security challenge is substantial and well-evidenced.

2. **Existing protections cover only a fraction of the risk.** The FY2025 NDAA restrictions apply to 3 of 17 DOE labs. The remaining 14 laboratories—conducting research in AI, quantum computing, energy storage, advanced materials, and other dual-use technologies—have no comparable statutory protections.

3. **Bipartisan momentum exists for expanded restrictions.** The GATE Act's predecessor passed the Senate Intelligence Committee 17-0, and the February 20 hearing demonstrated broad consensus on the need for enhanced security measures.

4. **Implementation challenges are significant.** DOE itself has warned that broad bans would significantly impact laboratory operations. The tension between security and scientific productivity has no easy resolution and will require ongoing Congressional oversight.

5. **New vulnerabilities are emerging.** The use of PRC-linked supercomputers by U.S. lab researchers demonstrates that security risks extend beyond physical access to digital collaboration channels that existing policies may not adequately address.
